Circuit breaker

A failing dependency is bad. A slow failing one is worse: every call sits waiting for a timeout, requests pile up behind it, and a service that was perfectly healthy falls over because something it depends on did. A circuit breaker counts the failures and, past a threshold, stops calling for a while. Callers fail instantly instead of slowly, and the dependency gets room to recover.

  1. Break the dependency with the controls on the right. Add errors or latency, or take it down entirely.
  2. Watch the breaker trip to OPEN. Calls stop reaching the dependency and start failing instantly instead.
  3. Make it healthy again and watch HALF-OPEN send a few probes before it trusts the dependency enough to close.

Dependency controls

The three states

CLOSED
Normal service. Calls go through, and consecutive failures are counted. Enough of them in a row trips the breaker.
OPEN
Nothing reaches the dependency. Every call fails immediately with 503, so callers get an answer in microseconds instead of waiting on a timeout that was going to fail anyway.
HALF-OPEN
After a cooldown, a couple of probes are allowed through to test the water. Enough successes and it closes; a single failure and it opens again for another cooldown.

The Breaker panel shows the thresholds this demo is using, and why it last changed state.

Why this matters

In a distributed system, one service failing is survivable. The danger is that failure spreading. Callers blocked on a dead dependency hold connections and memory, so they stop answering their own callers, who then block too. One slow database can take down a dozen services that never touched it.

Breaking the circuit stops that spread at the first hop. It also helps the dependency: a service struggling under load recovers much faster if you stop hammering it, which is what the cooldown is for.

The cost is honesty about uncertainty. While the breaker is open nobody knows whether the dependency is healthy, because nobody is asking. That is what 503 reports here: we didn't call it. The probes in HALF-OPEN are how the breaker finds out again, cheaply.
